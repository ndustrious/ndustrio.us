# Naming Brainstorm v5 — N, Spellable, Agency-Register

Fifth pass. Still N, with two new filters:

1. **Spellable from a single hearing in an ad.** Not "most people would probably get it" — actually unmissable.
2. **Reads like a modern design agency**, not a term-of-art firm.

Prior passes: v1 (retired), v2 (design vein), v3 (taste: Ligature/Stratum), v4 (N, term-of-art vein).

---

## What these filters change

The v3 taste — Latinate terms of art, borrowed from a discipline, cool and exact — is in genuine tension with both new constraints, and it's worth naming that plainly rather than pretending the passes all agree.

**Terms of art are obscure by construction.** That's what makes them feel precise, and it's exactly what makes them hard to spell on the radio. Narthex, Newel, Numen, Neume, and Nonpareil were all built on that trade-off. Under the new filter, **Narthex comes down** — I recommended it last pass, and "N-A-R-T-H-E-X" is not something a listener produces on the first hearing. The concept was the best part of v4 and the spelling was always its weak point; you've now made the spelling decisive.

**Modern design agencies name themselves the opposite way.** Look at what the field actually does: Instrument. Manual. Character. Method. Order. Field. Standard. Buck. Clay. Frog. Huge. Koto. Ueno. Gretel. Athletics. Work & Co.

Two patterns dominate, and neither is Latinate:

- **Short plain nouns, often deliberately small or humble things.** Frog. Buck. Clay. The name isn't a claim; it's an object, said flatly. The confidence comes from *not* explaining yourself.
- **Short invented or borrowed words with open vowels.** Koto, Ueno, Gretel, Mucho — easy in the mouth, easy on a page, meaning built by the work rather than carried by the word.

What's absent from both: anything that needs a footnote. **A modern agency name doesn't argue — it just sits there and lets the work argue.** That's a real shift from the Ligature/Narthex instinct, which wanted the name to carry the whole thesis.

The good news is that this makes the name's job smaller, and the story is still available — it just moves to the About page.

## Phase 4 — Finalist development

### 1. Nib — *the recommendation*

**Origin story:** A nib is the point of a pen — the few millimeters where pressure becomes a line and an intention becomes a mark someone else can read. Everything upstream of it is thinking. We're named for the place where it stops being thinking.

**Taglines:**
- *Where it becomes a mark.*
- *The point of contact.*
- *Thinking, made legible.*

**In the ad:** "That's where Nib comes in. They're seasoned builders — full-stack engineering paired with design and product expertise…" Three letters. A listener cannot get it wrong, and `nib.studio` is about as short as a spoken URL gets.

**Why it fits the agency register exactly:** Frog, Buck, Clay — the convention is a small, ordinary, specific object stated without explanation. Nib is that, and it's design-native without being *about* design in a way that excludes the engineering half. It also quietly does the craft work the whole brand rests on: a nib is the tool of someone who makes marks by hand and cares how they look.

**Alongside Stratum:** "Stratum, by Nib." A short Anglo object under a Latin structural singular. Different in kind, which reads as parent-and-product rather than sibling-and-sibling. Works.

**Watch for:**
- **nib Group is an ASX-listed Australian health insurer** — different category, different hemisphere, no realistic trademark conflict in Class 42 US, but it dominates a bare search for the word. Own a domain that always carries context.
- It's small and quiet. That's the convention working as intended, but if you want the name itself to make an argument, Nib won't — the About page has to.

**Domain strategy:** `nib.com` is out of reach. `nib.studio`, `nib.design`, `nibstudio.com`. The short TLD is a positioning signal and solves the search-context problem at once.

---

### 2. Newline — *the synthesis pick*

Across five passes this is the only word that is a real term of art in **both** disciplines at once, under the same name and meaning the same thing. In typography, the newline ends a line and begins a fresh one. In code, `\n` does precisely that. Designers and engineers both use the word daily and neither thinks of it as borrowed from the other.

For a firm whose entire differentiator is that design and engineering aren't a handoff, that's not a metaphor for the thesis — it's an instance of it.

**Origin story:** A newline is the character that ends one line and starts the next. It's the same word, meaning the same thing, in typography and in code — one of the few things both halves of a product team already share. We took it as the name because it's what we are.

**Taglines:** *Design and code, same word.* · *Start the next line.* · *One team, one language.*

**Watch for:** newline.co is a developer-education company — check Class 41/42. And it reads a touch more technical than Nib, which cuts against "design agency" slightly. If your partner is the one whose taste governs the mark, ask them which of these two they'd rather set in type.

---

### 3. Nara — *the purest agency-register play*

Koto and Ueno made borrowed Japanese place names a live convention in this exact field, and Nara is the best of the unclaimed ones: Japan's ancient capital, a city of temples and sacred craft still standing after thirteen centuries. Two open syllables, effortless to say, impossible to misspell, and it looks good set in almost any typeface — which is a real consideration and the one your design partner will feel first.

**Watch for:** it carries no meaning in English, so the name is a blank the work has to fill. And it borrows a culture with no connection to yours — an accepted convention in the field, but some will read it as decorative. Worth a candid conversation rather than a footnote.

---

### 4. North — *the best concept, and probably unavailable*

True north: the fixed reference that doesn't move, which you navigate everything else against. For a firm whose stated standard is *good, beautiful, and true*, no other N word states the thesis so plainly in one syllable.

North is also a well-regarded London brand agency. That's a direct in-category collision — the highest risk classification in the naming framework — and it's the reason this sits fourth instead of first despite the best concept in the pass. Different market and no US trademark presence I'm aware of, so it's a judgment call rather than a hard stop, but it's your partner's judgment to make with clear eyes.

---

## Recommendation

**Nib**, with **Newline** as the close alternate.

Nib satisfies every filter simultaneously: three letters and unmissable on air, squarely in the modern agency convention, design-native without excluding engineering, and short enough that the spoken URL in a podcast ad costs nothing. Newline makes a sharper argument about *what your firm is* and is the one to take if your design partner prefers a name that says something.

One thing to settle before choosing: **decide whether you want the name to carry the thesis or the work to carry it.** Ligature, Armature, and Narthex all wanted the name to make the argument. The modern agency convention you've now asked for is built on the opposite premise — the name sits there quietly and the portfolio does the talking. Both are legitimate. They just produce different shortlists, and the last four passes were optimizing for the first one.
